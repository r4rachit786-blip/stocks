## Facts
- Sweet orange = pomelo x mandarin hybrid, from southern China / north-east India / Myanmar (Wu et al., Nature 2018).
- Bitter (Seville) orange brought to Spain by the Arabs/Moors around the 10th century; mostly perfume, medicine and marmalade, not eating.
- Portuguese brought sweet oranges from Asia in the 1500s. Greek portokali, Turkish portakal, Arabic burtuqal.
- Louis XIV: over 1,000 orange trees at the Versailles Orangerie; silver orange-tree tubs in the Hall of Mirrors (melted down in 1689).
- Columbus brought citrus seeds to Hispaniola on his second voyage (1493).
- Citrus against scurvy: James Lind's 1747 trial.
- Oranges as rare Christmas treats in Europe and America.
- Tropical oranges stay green when ripe: the peel needs cool nights to lose its chlorophyll. Some Florida oranges get Citrus Red No. 2 on the peel (FDA-permitted).

## Facts to keep straight
- Path: Sanskrit naranga (likely Dravidian origin) > Persian narang > Arabic naranj > Spanish naranja, Italian narancia/arancia > Old French orenge > English orange.
- The lost n: "une narange" heard as "une orange" (rebracketing); Italian lost it too (arancia).
- The "or" (gold) influence on the French form is a common theory, hence "some say".
- English used "geoluhread" (yellow-red) in Old English. Robin redbreast, red fox and red hair keep the older usage.
- Colour name: commonly cited first record about 1502 (cloth bought for Margaret Tudor). The fruit word is earlier (14th century).
- Greek portokali, also Romanian portocală and Arabic burtuqal, after the Portuguese, who brought sweet oranges in the 1500s.
- Correction for v2: the lost n most likely happened in Italian (una narancia > un'arancia), then French orenge, possibly shaped by "or". Ripe oranges in the tropics often stay green: the peel only turns orange after cool nights.
